Chapter I

House Crow

Eighteen years later, a young man named Kael of the Gutter District stole a magistrate’s signet ring without breaking stride.

He did not know he was anything but a thief.

He only knew he was fast.

The streets of Winterhold were narrow and hungry. Smoke from coal braziers clung to the cold air, and laundry lines crisscrossed overhead like snares. Nestled high within the shadow of the Frostveil Mountains, Winterhold was one of the oldest surviving cities in the Aerie Dominion. Long before Houses rose to power and kings sat upon the throne, the city itself had been carved by dwarven hands directly into the living stone of the mountains.

Few dwarves remained.

Yet their presence lingered in every wall, every tunnel and every echoing hall beneath the city.

The mountain remembered its makers, even if the world had largely forgotten them.

Kael moved through it all with easy grace — unnoticed, unremarkable.

Until he chose not to be.

That is my target, Kael thought. The magistrate matched the Guild of Magpies’ sketch: pencil moustache, sunken eyes, wispy red hair, a round belly and chains of office. Two Swans followed close behind. The magistrate belonged to no House, but the escort told Kael whose interests he served.

The magistrate entered the temple district, where six sanctuaries rose from Winterhold’s mountain stone. Kael tracked him past Aurelian’s raven tower, the severe fortress of Vulturis and the white bridges of Cygnara; the hidden doors of Corvax and the blade-like towers of Falcoris vanished behind frozen mist, while the sixth shrine watched from higher ground. Gods were said to protect the Dominion from places like these. Kael would have settled for one keeping his fingers warm.

A frozen waterfall climbed the wall beside the temple steps. Kael caught his reflection in the ice and stopped for half a heartbeat.

He looked older than he remembered. His first clear memory was of the Gutter District in Seraphis City: six or seven years old, starving beneath an archway until Mr. Value found him. The Bowerbirds fed him, trained him and gave him a strip of wall in their leaking den. Kael’s talent eventually earned the gang a place in the Guild of Magpies—and earned Kael the privilege of taking the Guild’s most dangerous assignments.

This assignment had waited beneath a loose stone at the base of a magpie statue: travel to Winterhold, steal the magistrate’s ring and ask no questions.

Near-black curls whipped around Kael’s lean face. He had always thought his eyes emerald, although violet moved through them when the light shifted. He stood a little over six feet, broad-shouldered but lean from missed meals, with the balanced posture of someone accustomed to rooftops and sleeping where danger could reach him.

His clothes were slightly better than they had been in earlier years, but they were still little more than a journeyman traveller's rags: dark, dirty grey, with a small copper magpie pin attached to the ragged cloak.

He stopped gawking at himself as the magistrate headed up the temple steps. Kael quickly scaled the wall beside them and reached the top to head him off.

He passed the magistrate on the temple steps, dipped in close as if to beg, brushed the man’s sleeve—and kept walking.

The weight of gold warmed his palm.

Behind him, outrage erupted.

Kael grinned and ran.

He vaulted crates, slid beneath a cart, kicked off a wall, and caught a hanging banner to swing across an alley gap. The city blurred around him.

He was almost impressed when they caught him.

The net dropped from above — weighted, precise. It pinned him before he hit the cobblestones.

“Not bad,” said a voice overhead.

Kael struggled, but the rope tightened.

A figure dropped lightly beside him, dressed in black leathers stitched with dark feathers. A silver insignia gleamed at his collar: a stylised crow in flight.

“You’ve been observed,” he said.

“That’s usually bad for business,” Kael muttered.

“For common thieves,” he agreed. “Yes.”

He crouched.

“For candidates, it’s an invitation.”

Kael looked up at the figure, shocked yet intrigued.

The dark figure crouched beside him and pulled back his hood. He was tall and spare beneath the feather-stitched leathers, with iron-grey hair swept away from a rugged face crossed by old blade scars. One pale seam cut through his left brow and disappeared into his temple. His eyes were the colour of storm clouds, but when he leaned closer, violet glimmered in their depths. He smelled faintly of rain, leather and woodsmoke, and held himself with the unnerving stillness of a man who had already measured every escape.

“My name is Grey; come with me and join our creed or face the noose. The choice is yours.”

Kael gave a slight smirk and looked around as though weighing his options. There were none—not if he waited for someone else to save him. Mr. Value would never risk the Bowerbirds for one captured thief, and the city watch already filled the street behind Grey. Yet the Crow had called him a candidate rather than a prisoner. Whatever waited beneath that black hood, Kael decided he would enter it on his own terms, learn what the Crows wanted and find his own way out if necessary.

Kael sighed. “Well, it seems like I don’t have much of a choice, does it?”

The net snapped loose and vanished upwards before Kael could move. Grey pulled a black hood over his head.

Kael could distinguish hoofbeats, boots and the creak of leather, but nothing more. Someone hauled him onto a horse and forced his arms around the rider in front of him. Grey, perhaps. Kael hoped so; the alternatives seemed worse.

They galloped through the city. Gates swung open, more horses joined them and still no one spoke.

Exhaustion eventually overcame suspicion. Kael stopped fighting the weight of his eyelids and drifted into an uneasy sleep.
